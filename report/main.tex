\begin{abstract}
RoleKV uses activated LoRA (aLoRA) to share key-value (KV) caches between a
Coder and a Reviewer, rebuilding each role's adapted states at handoff.
We evaluate six methods using Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct. On 100 MBPP tasks
with test-suite feedback, this H1 policy reduces mean processed prefill
tokens by 68.7\% and median switch time to first token from 139 to 45\,ms

59, without establishing quality superiority or equivalence. Fixed four-turn

prefill tokens as context grows from 1.1k to 8.1k, while summed model-turn
time changes from 4\% slower to 13\% faster. Shared-adapter and mismatched-cache
baselines remain competitive. Among H1's 41 second-round approvals, 35
programs fail the suite. The result is a measured gain in prefill volume
and switch responsiveness, with a review failure that limits the
interpretation of closed-loop time savings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In a Coder--Reviewer loop, one role writes a program and another inspects
the code and execution feedback before requesting a revision.
Execution-guided refinement can improve generated programs
\cite{chen2023selfdebug}, but every attempt adds history for both roles to
read. LoRA adapters specialize these roles on a shared backbone
\cite{hu2022lora}. Sharing weights does not guarantee compatible key-value
(KV) states: the same text can produce different caches under different
adapters \cite{greenewald2025alora}. Our full-recomputation baseline therefore
reprocesses the accumulated history under the incoming role's adapter.

We build RoleKV on activated LoRA (aLoRA), an existing mechanism that leaves
states before an invocation unchanged \cite{greenewald2025alora}. Each
role's adapter activates at its header. At the next handoff, our H1 policy
rebuilds the previous role's adapted span with base weights, preserving one
shared base-model prefix (Figure~\ref{fig:cache-handoff}). H2 skips that
reconstruction and keeps mixed-adapter states. The engineering challenge
is to preserve activation and cache semantics through decoding while
accounting for the work saved and the work added at handoff.

Our question is \emph{when does cross-adapter KV sharing reduce model cost
without compromising coding-task quality?} A long common prefix creates
more repeated prefill, while decoding and handoff reconstruction can offset
the saving. A premature approval can also make a session cheaper by stopping
it early. We separate these effects through three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An instrumented runtime:} six cache/adapter policies,
    explicit aLoRA activation through decoding, and logged token accounting,
    including handoff reconstruction, used by a replay dashboard.
  \item \textbf{Two complementary evaluations:} fixed four-turn traces
    across four context lengths, and 600 closed-loop sessions on 100 paired
    MBPP tasks \cite{austin2021mbpp} using five trained adapters.
  \item \textbf{Benefits and failure modes:} large prefill and
    switch-latency gains coexist with modest context-dependent model-time
    gains, competitive simpler baselines, and a second-round review failure.
\end{itemize}
Our contribution is the implementation and empirical study of these trade-offs.
The underlying aLoRA sharing mechanism comes from prior work.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Code Repair and Shared History}
Iterative code repair revisits a task together with earlier programs and their
execution results. Self-Debugging uses generation, explanation, feedback, and
revision to improve code with an existing language model
\cite{chen2023selfdebug}. Its MBPP evaluation exposes one test and reserves
two for final scoring: execution feedback can reveal a failure, while passing
the visible test leaves further errors undetected. This motivates a
Coder--Reviewer loop in which both roles inspect a growing common history.
The benefit of separate role adapters remains an empirical question, since a
single model can also perform generation and review.

\subsection{LoRA and KV Compatibility}
LoRA specializes a frozen backbone through trainable low-rank weight updates
\cite{hu2022lora}. S-LoRA makes many such adapters practical to serve through
heterogeneous batching and unified memory management for adapter weights and
KV tensors \cite{sheng2024slora}. These optimizations exploit shared backbone
weights; cross-adapter KV reuse additionally requires compatible activations.
An adapter can change cached keys and values directly through projection
updates or indirectly through hidden states produced by earlier layers
\cite{greenewald2025alora}.

Ordinary autoregressive caching retains earlier keys and values during
decoding. vLLM's PagedAttention manages these tensors in blocks and supports
sharing within and across requests \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}. With an
unchanged model and compatible positions, identical prefixes can reuse their
states. Identical text under different adapters does not generally satisfy
that condition. The resulting trade-off depends on cache policy: full
recomputation uses one live cache, whereas private warm caches retain a prefix
for each role and compute its missing history on return. Cross-adapter sharing
must therefore be assessed against both recomputation cost and the memory
required to keep private caches resident.

\subsection{Exact Cross-Adapter Sharing}
Activated LoRA (aLoRA) preserves base-model states before an invocation
sequence and applies adaptation from that sequence onward
\cite{greenewald2025alora}. This enables exact reuse of the base-processed
prefix. Li et al. integrate the mechanism into vLLM through base-aligned block
hashing and activation-aware masking \cite{li2025aloraserving}. The boundary
is important for sequential role invocations: the aLoRA paper notes that
adapter-generated states cannot be directly reused by another adapter or the
base model, so the generated text must be prefilled again. Our H1 policy
applies this recomputation at handoff, including the adapted role header.

ICaRus takes a different approach: it freezes a shared logical encoder that
writes KV states and trains task-specific logical decoders that read them
\cite{woo2026icarus}. During generation, the base and adapted paths run
together, with shared weight reads and combined attention queries reducing
their execution overhead. Thus, ICaRus maintains reusable states throughout
decoding, whereas H1 reconstructs them in a contiguous pass at handoff.
ICaRus evaluates code-generation quality and HotpotQA-based serving workloads;
those experiments do not directly measure our code-repair loop. In both
architectures, exact cache reuse concerns states under the chosen adaptation
design; task quality relative to conventional LoRA requires separate evaluation.

\subsection{Approximate Sharing and Reconstruction}
Other approaches reuse representations across model variants while managing
the resulting mismatch. DroidSpeak selectively recomputes layers of another
variant's cache and reuses the rest \cite{liu2026droidspeak}. LRAgent instead
decomposes the value cache into a shared base component and a compact
adapter-dependent component \cite{jeon2026lragent}. BaseShared maintains
per-agent low-rank caches for standard adapters; BaseLRShared further shares
the low-rank cache using a shared-$A$ adapter architecture. Sharing the base
weights alone does not make the base component identical, because its input
hidden states can still depend on the producing adapter.

ForkKV combines disaggregated shared and agent-specific caches with a
DualRadixTree and a reconstruction kernel for multi-LoRA serving
\cite{wang2026forkkv}. PReCache precomputes each adapter's low-rank cache;
its ReBaseShared variant additionally constructs a neutral base cache from
adapter-free hidden states \cite{jeon2026precache}. The lazy-prefill schedule
performs this reconstruction after an agent's turn. This resembles H1 in
scheduling base-state reconstruction at a handoff, but serves a different
purpose: PReCache approximates standard adapters' representations, whereas
H1 restores the base prefix expected by the incoming aLoRA. These distinctions
separate the choice of reusable representation from the cost of maintaining it.

\subsection{Scope and Measurement}
Prior work establishes exact and approximate paths to cross-adapter sharing.
We evaluate aLoRA handoffs in a small-model Coder--Reviewer loop, including
the cost of reconstruction and decoding. Fixed transcripts isolate systems
cost, while closed-loop runs reveal changes in generated code, review
decisions, and session length. PReCache also distinguishes controlled
efficiency traces from actual trajectories \cite{jeon2026precache}.
Our baselines test full recomputation, one shared adapter, and mismatched
reuse; the other systems reviewed here provide context rather than direct
experimental comparisons.

\section{Research Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How does shared-context length affect prefill volume, switch latency,
    and model-turn time when the conversation is fixed?
  \item How do cache handoff policies affect task quality and model cost?
  \item Do per-role adapters and exact sharing offer a measured advantage
    over one shared adapter or mismatched-cache reuse in this workload?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods and Experimental Design}
\label{sec:methods}
We use two complementary experiments: fixed-output traces that hold behavior
constant, and closed-loop evaluation in which each method generates its own
trajectory. Training records, event logs, and their hashes are indexed in
\path{report/methods_manifest.json} and \path{report/results_summary.json}.
All primary results use an RTX 4090 Laptop GPU with 16\,GB under WSL2, bf16
inference, greedy decoding, and the same model snapshot
\texttt{2e1fd397}. The recorded environment uses PyTorch 2.14.1+cu126,
Transformers 5.20.1, and PEFT 0.21.2. An additional 4070 base-model run is
excluded from the primary comparison.

The \href{https://github.com/Yinghao-Guan/ai-hackathon-26}{project repository}
includes the runtime, training and evaluation scripts, recorded runs, and a
dashboard that replays the event logs. The offline report script verifies
paired task sets and accounting totals and regenerates the result tables
and plots without invoking the model.

\subsection{Tasks and Shared Context}
The evaluated model is Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct
\cite{hui2024qwen25coder}; the 0.5B variant is used for development. The task
loader uses MBPP's \texttt{full} configuration \cite{austin2021mbpp}, pooling
the train, validation, and prompt splits for data generation and selecting
100 test-split tasks with Python's random sampler at seed 0. It checks that
the training and selected evaluation task IDs are disjoint.

Each TASK segment contains a repository-style \texttt{utils.py}, the task
description, and its first assert. The context builder samples 30 reference
solutions from the pooled training tasks by default, excluding the target
task; selection is deterministic from a hash of the task ID. These functions
provide shared text to process, not evidence that the task requires a real
repository. In the current SFT exports, TASK segments range from 1,276 to
2,497 development-tokenizer tokens, with a median of approximately 1,809.
For the 100 evaluated tasks, logged TASK lengths range from 1,280 to 2,710
tokens, with a median of 1,804. The added context and evaluation subset are
study-specific augmentations.

\subsection{Teacher Trajectories and SFT Examples}
The generator requests a solution or an intentionally faulty first attempt
from a teacher, executes the code, and retains a trajectory only if its final
code passes the full assert suite. A requested faulty attempt must fail that
suite; its feedback is generated from the real test output, followed by a
tested repair. Passing attempts receive a deterministic \texttt{APPROVE}
target. The configured teacher identifier is \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}; the
export does not record the API model revision or generation arguments, so
these remain provenance information to recover from the generation run.

The current export contains 280 accepted trajectories from distinct tasks:
222 Coder--Reviewer sequences and 58 Coder--Reviewer--Coder repair sequences.
They yield 338 Coder and 280 Reviewer SFT rows, with 222 \texttt{APPROVE} and
58 \texttt{REVISE} labels. The Coder file retains the 58 faulty initial
attempts as targets as well as their repairs; it represents trajectory
imitation, not exclusively correct-solution supervision. No Reviewer turn
after a successful repair is included in these three-turn trajectories.
The accepted distribution therefore differs from the generator's nominal
50\% bug-request default.

Prompts use separately tokenized ChatML segments. The system segment is shared
with inference, and a role's complete assistant header is its aLoRA invocation
sequence. Labels mask all context tokens with $-100$ and supervise only the
target BODY, including its closing delimiter. The two files' median lengths
are 2,036 and 2,060.5 tokens for Coder and Reviewer, respectively; the maximum
is 2,932. These lengths were checked locally using the development tokenizer.
Full data hashes appear in the manifest and \texttt{data/README.md}.

\subsection{Session Protocol and Test Feedback}
The runtime follows Coder $\rightarrow$ tool output $\rightarrow$ Reviewer.
It uses greedy decoding with default limits of 512 new Coder tokens and
128 Reviewer tokens, stopping on ChatML end-of-turn or end-of-text tokens.
An \texttt{APPROVE} or \texttt{INVALID} Reviewer verdict ends the session.
After the second \texttt{REVISE}, the next Coder output is final, without a
further tool call or review, for at most five role turns. Tests execute in a
temporary-directory Python subprocess with a five-second timeout and an
output cap of 2,000 characters.

\paragraph{Feedback boundary.}
Although only the first assert appears in the TASK text, the current data
generator and default evaluation runner execute \emph{all} MBPP asserts in
the tool call and return the resulting stdout and stderr to the model.
Final code is scored against the same suite. These are test-suite-assisted
repair results, not evaluation on tests withheld throughout the interaction;
failure output can reveal additional assertions. A runner option restricts
feedback to the first assert, matching the visibility boundary used by
Self-Debugging \cite{chen2023selfdebug}, but that is a separate protocol whose
runs must be labeled and analyzed separately. Comparisons must hold the
feedback policy fixed, and equal feedback access does not establish
generalization to unseen tests.

\subsection{Cache Policies and Correctness}
The implemented runtime uses one logical token history and one
\texttt{DynamicCache} per session, with the following policies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A2 (full recomputation):} discard the cache at each turn and
    prefill the entire history and new role header under the incoming role's
    standard LoRA.
  \item \textbf{A5-H1 (canonicalize):} retain the base-processed prefix, crop
    the previous role's adapted span, and rebuild it with base weights at
    the start of the next turn. This covers the previous role's header and
    output. New tool and boundary tokens are processed in the same forward;
    the incoming aLoRA activates only at its own header. The final session
    turn is never canonicalized because there is no subsequent handoff.
  \item \textbf{A5-H2 (keep):} retain the previous role's adapted KV, process
    new input, and activate the incoming aLoRA at its header. The resulting
    history contains states produced by different adapters.
\end{itemize}
All aLoRA decode steps receive explicit activation offsets so that adaptation
remains active when the input chunk no longer contains the invocation header.
A0 uses the base model, A1 uses one shared standard LoRA, and A3 retains
mixed-adapter history with standard role LoRAs; these policies also have
runtime implementations. H1 and H2 use the same aLoRA weights. A0 and A1
retain their compatible single-model cache; A3 switches standard role LoRAs
without rebuilding the previous role's states.

Inference uses PEFT 0.21.2 with scaled dot-product attention. A contiguous-slice
aLoRA path and persistent activation-offset hooks replace masked row selection
and per-call hook registration. Timings characterize this optimized runtime;
correctness checks cover activation behavior, cache restoration, positions,
and token accounting.

Private warm caches remain unimplemented in the inspected runtime. The
proposed policy keeps a valid prefix per standard role adapter and computes
missing history on return. Its comparison with A2 must use identical adapters
and count all resident caches. Figure~\ref{fig:cache-handoff} illustrates
policies independently of checkpoint quality.

The test suite compares cached logits with a full forward under the
\emph{same aLoRA}, and canonicalized KV with a fresh base-model forward.
These tests use the 0.5B development model and nonzero random rank-8 adapters.
The recorded pre-evaluation gate passed 60/60 tests in fp32 on CUDA. Four bf16
equivalence comparisons failed their tolerances in the development tests,
as documented in the run notes. Consequently, exact sharing describes H1's
representation invariant, not bitwise equality of all bf16 schedules or
completed equivalence validation of the trained 1.5B deployment. Ordinary
LoRA is a task-quality reference rather than H1's equivalence target. H2
retains mixed states and intentionally does not satisfy the H1 invariant.

\subsection{Fixed-Trace System Measurements}
The cost sweep replays one four-turn conversation: faulty Coder output,
\texttt{REVISE}, repaired Coder output, and \texttt{APPROVE}, with fixed tool
text. It generates 197 tokens per session, including stop tokens, and makes
three role switches. We vary TASK length across 1,101, 2,100, 4,095, and 8,114
tokens, comparing A2, H1, and H2 over five repeats each. Methods are
interleaved after warm-up in a fixed order. The sweep uses untrained adapters
at the measured ranks and precision, so it measures execution cost rather
than learned behavior. Its forced \texttt{passed} flag denotes replay
completion and is excluded from quality analysis. Output length and switch
count are fixed; their independent effects are not evaluated.

Processed prefill tokens sum new prefill and canonicalization tokens, counting
every non-decoding token position fed to the model. This is a token-volume
metric rather than measured FLOPs. Switch TTFT is the median over every turn
after turn zero, including H1 reconstruction. Model-turn time sums synchronized
turn durations, including decoding and host overhead inside each turn, but
excludes tool execution, final scoring, and model loading. We report median
session time and its observed range across the five repeats. The logged
\texttt{peak\_kv\_mb} is the maximum logical KV tensor size at turn boundaries,
not peak device allocation; we make no measured memory-saving claim.

\subsection{Quality and Reviewer Behavior}
Each of the six methods generates one greedy trajectory for each of the same
100 tasks. The log contains all 600 final task outcomes and 1,923 model turns.
We report final-code full-suite pass counts, mean turn counts, generated token
counts, and invalid or truncated outputs, retaining stopped and failed
sessions. The code may undergo up to two revisions, so the final task pass
rate is a session-level success measure rather than single-attempt Coder
accuracy; we avoid interpreting the source summaries' \texttt{pass@1} label
as unrevised code-generation performance.

For second-round reviews, we count approvals and the fraction whose final
code fails the suite. An approval terminates the session without changing
the reviewed code, so the final outcome scores that approval directly.
This denominator includes all second-round approvals, without conditioning
on first-assert success. No second review occurs in the SFT trajectories.
Initial accuracy, repair, regression, and visible-test-conditioned approval
rates require scoring intermediate code, whose outcomes are not stored in
the event log; they are outside this analysis.

\subsection{Cost--Quality Interpretation}
Closed-loop methods take different trajectories, so fewer processed tokens
can reflect premature approval or failure rather than a better cache policy.
We interpret cost jointly with task success and Reviewer behavior, using a
paired A2--H1 success comparison. We report Wilson 95\% intervals for their
individual success proportions and an exact two-sided McNemar test on
discordant tasks. These quantify task-level sampling uncertainty; they do
not measure variation across training seeds or decoding runs. A small observed
gap does not establish equivalence. Tool-inclusive task wall times are not
stored, so every reported time comparison concerns model turns.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Fixed Traces: Savings Depend on Context}
Figure~\ref{fig:cost-scaling} separates prefill volume, role-switch latency,
and summed model-turn time while holding generated text constant. H1 processes
1,576 versus A2's 5,599 prefill tokens at 1.1k context, and 8,589 versus
33,651 at 8.1k: reductions of 3.55$\times$ and 3.92$\times$, respectively.
Every H1 session includes 221 canonicalization tokens. H2 avoids those
221 tokens, but retains adapted states rather than restoring the base prefix.
The growing ratio follows from amortizing the shared prefix over four turns;
it does not imply that all model computation is reduced by the same factor.

A2's median switch TTFT rises from 94 to 583\,ms, whereas H1 stays between
47 and 50\,ms across the four contexts. The resulting latency ratio grows
from 1.89$\times$ to 11.71$\times$. This is the clearest measured benefit
of sharing: the incoming role avoids reprocessing a growing common prefix.

The model-time benefit is substantially smaller. At 1.1k, H1 takes 6.84\,s
versus 6.56\,s for A2, about 4\% slower. At 2.1k the medians are 6.89 and
6.82\,s. H1 becomes slightly faster at 4.1k (7.01 versus 7.19\,s), and is
13\% faster at 8.1k (7.32 versus 8.40\,s). At that longest context, observed
session ranges are 7.16--7.55\,s for H1 and 8.36--8.46\,s for A2. Median
decode time is approximately 34.2--34.4\,ms per token for H1, compared with
31.5--31.9\,ms for A2. Residual aLoRA execution overhead therefore offsets
some prefill savings. The crossover is specific to this trace, hardware,
adapter configuration, and implementation; it is not a universal context
threshold.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Quality and Model Cost}
Table~\ref{tab:eval-summary} reports all six methods on the same 100 tasks.
H1 passes 61 tasks and A2 passes 59. Paired by task, both pass 51, only A2
passes 8, only H1 passes 10, and neither passes 31. The exact two-sided
McNemar $p$-value is 0.815. Individual Wilson 95\% intervals are
49.2--68.1\% for A2 and 51.2--70.0\% for H1. These results provide no
detected success-rate difference on this sample, but do not establish
equivalence or non-inferiority.

H1 averages 2,197 processed prefill tokens per task, including 138
canonicalization tokens, versus 7,013 for A2: 68.7\% fewer. Median switch
TTFT falls from 139.4 to 45.4\,ms, a 3.07$\times$ ratio. Mean model-turn
time falls from 5.56 to 4.29\,s, or 22.8\%. This last reduction combines
cache behavior with different generated trajectories. H1 averages 2.94
turns and 124.8 generated tokens, compared with A2's 3.27 turns and 170.2
tokens. It therefore cannot be attributed wholly to caching. The fixed
replay provides the cleaner systems comparison.

The other baselines also constrain the interpretation. A1, which uses one
shared standard LoRA and a compatible cache, passes 61 tasks with 2,060
prefill tokens per task. A3, which retains mismatched per-role LoRA states,
passes 62 with 2,061 prefill tokens. H2 passes 60, with lower prefill volume
than H1 but the same second-round review weakness discussed below. Thus,
this workload does not demonstrate a quality advantage for per-role
adapters over a shared adapter, or for exact over mismatched cache reuse.
The mechanistic distinction between H1 and A3 remains important, but it
does not substitute for an observed task-quality advantage.

\subsection{Second-Round Review Failure}
Table~\ref{tab:reviewer-summary} reveals a failure hidden by aggregate task
pass rates. H1 approves 41 of 45 second reviews; 35 of those 41 approvals
(85.4\%) concern code that fails the suite. H2 approves 39 of 45, including
34 failures (87.2\%). A2 approves only 2 of 43 second reviews, and neither
approved program fails; A1 and A3 likewise have no failing second-round
approvals in these runs. Zero observed errors on their small approval
denominators should not be read as guaranteed reviewer reliability.

\section{Limitations and Discussion}
\subsection{What the Experiments Establish}
The answer depends on what is held fixed. With identical generated text,
longer shared context increases H1's advantage in switch latency, while
decode overhead limits total model-time savings. With generated
trajectories, similar final pass counts conceal different review decisions
and opportunities for repair. Finally, A1 and A3 remain competitive, so
per-role specialization and exact sharing have no demonstrated quality
premium in this benchmark.

H1's base-prefix invariant provides a clear cache contract for independently
trained role adapters. Its engineering value and the measured prefill savings
stand alongside the quality and numerical-validation limits described above;
they do not establish that H1 is the best quality--cost policy overall.

\subsection{Coverage and Measurement Limits}
The study uses one 1.5B backbone, two roles, one training seed, and one
greedy run per method on 100 function-level tasks. The added reference
functions create shared context but do not turn MBPP into repository-level
software engineering. Full-suite tool feedback further limits comparison
with protocols that withhold tests. Different ranks and parameter counts
also prevent attributing quality differences solely to cache policy when
comparing standard LoRA with aLoRA.

The fixed sweep uses one synthetic transcript and untrained adapters.
Five timing repeats describe local variation, not run-to-run variation
across machines or sessions with different text. The closed-loop runs
execute methods in separate blocks rather than repeated randomized order.
All primary timings are from one laptop GPU under WSL2. The separate
4070 A0 result is 54/100 rather than the 4090's 51/100, reinforcing the need
to keep the main comparison on one machine. We do not report tool-inclusive
wall time, measured peak GPU
memory savings, or an energy reduction.

Private warm caches are illustrated but not implemented or timed. Output
length, role count, and switch frequency are not independently swept.
Exact sharing may lose its time advantage when role outputs become longer,
because reconstruction grows with the adapted span, but this dependence
has not been measured here. The current evidence cannot locate a general
break-even boundary against a memory-resident private cache.

\subsection{Next Experiments}
The first priority is to add multi-round review trajectories and repeat
evaluation with several training seeds, while separately scoring initial
code, repairs, and regressions. A matched-weight full-recomputation aLoRA
baseline at the deployed precision would isolate H1's cache handling from
adapter training and strengthen numerical validation. A factorial trace
sweep over output length and switch count, an implemented private-cache
baseline, and tool-inclusive timing would then test the remaining cost
questions. Evaluations with tests withheld from feedback and larger,
repository-level workloads are needed before generalizing the quality
findings beyond this setup.

\section{Conclusion}
Cross-adapter KV sharing reduces repeated prefill and role-switch latency
in the RoleKV Coder--Reviewer runtime. On fixed four-turn traces, H1's switch
latency stays near 50\,ms while full recomputation grows to 583\,ms at
8.1k context; model-time savings emerge only as the shared prefix becomes
large enough to offset decoding overhead. On 100 closed-loop tasks, H1
passes 61 versus A2's 59, with no detected paired success-rate difference,
but frequent false approvals in second reviews weaken the interpretation
of its lower task cost. Shared-adapter and mismatched-cache baselines are
also competitive. The practical lesson is to measure reconstruction,
decoding, and review behavior together: a lower prefill count is most useful
when it reduces a substantial part of the session's work, and a lower
task cost must be interpreted alongside how the session ended.
